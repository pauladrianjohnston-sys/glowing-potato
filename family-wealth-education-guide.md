# A Guide for Parents and Grandparents

## Supporting Young Adults Receiving Family Wealth

*For the giving generation: understanding the terrain before you act*

---

## How to Use This Guide

This document is designed to help you—the parents, grandparents, and extended family members—prepare for one of the most consequential gifts you can give: not just wealth, but the conditions for your young adult children or grandchildren to develop a healthy relationship with that wealth.

**Read this before** designing any education, having "the conversation," or making assumptions about what your young adults need.

**Expect to feel uncomfortable** at points. Some of what follows may challenge well-intentioned instincts. That discomfort is a feature, not a bug.

---

## Part 1: Core Concepts You Need to Understand

### 1.1 Emotional Debt

**What it is:** The felt obligation to repay a gift with gratitude, compliance, or life choices that please the giver—even when no such expectation is stated.

**Why it matters:** When young adults receive significant wealth, they often feel they "owe" the giver something beyond thanks. This can manifest as:
- Pursuing careers they think will please you
- Hiding financial decisions they fear you'll disapprove of
- Performing gratitude rather than feeling it
- Avoiding you to escape the weight of obligation

**The trap:** The more generous the gift, the heavier the felt debt. The more "helpful" the accompanying education or guidance, the more the young person may feel surveilled and constrained.

> "The greatest gift can become the heaviest chain when it comes with the giver's gaze still attached."

### 1.2 Psychological Safety vs. Performative Compliance

**Psychological safety** means a person can be honest—including about confusion, mistakes, or disagreement—without fear of judgment, punishment, or disappointing someone they love.

**Performative compliance** means saying and doing the "right things" to satisfy the perceived expectations of authority figures, while internal experience is entirely different.

**The risk you face:** Young adults are highly skilled at performing compliance. They have spent their entire lives learning what adults want to hear. When family and money are involved, the incentive to perform increases dramatically.

**How to tell the difference:**
| Genuine Engagement | Performative Compliance |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| Asks challenging questions | Asks confirming questions ("So I should...?") |
| Expresses uncertainty or confusion | Expresses confident agreement |
| Sometimes disagrees or pushes back | Always aligns with your framing |
| Shares struggles and mistakes | Shares only successes |
| Engages inconsistently (human) | Engages perfectly (suspicious) |

### 1.3 Differentiation of Self

From family systems theory (Murray Bowen): **Differentiation** is the ability to maintain your own identity, values, and decisions while remaining emotionally connected to your family.

**Low differentiation** looks like:
- Making decisions to please or rebel against family (both are reactive)
- Difficulty knowing what *you* actually want vs. what family expects
- Emotional fusion (your feelings depend on family's approval)
- Cutting off from family to escape pressure

**High differentiation** looks like:
- Clear sense of personal values, even when family disagrees
- Ability to stay connected without being controlled
- Tolerating family's disappointment without collapsing
- Making autonomous decisions while maintaining warmth

**Your goal:** Support differentiation. This means your young adults might make choices you wouldn't make—and that's success, not failure.

### 1.4 The Wealth-Giver's Dilemma

You face an inherent contradiction:
- You want to help → but helping can create dependency
- You want to guide → but guidance can impose your values
- You want to protect → but protection can prevent learning
- You want to stay connected → but involvement can feel like surveillance

**There is no clean solution.** The best path involves accepting that:
1. You cannot control the outcome
2. Your young adults may make "mistakes" (by your definition)
3. Staying connected matters more than being right
4. Your job is to offer, not to ensure uptake

### 1.5 Money Scripts

From the work of financial psychologists Brad Klontz and Ted Klontz: **Money scripts** are unconscious beliefs about money formed in childhood that drive adult financial behavior.

**Four common categories:**
| Script | Belief | Risk |
|--------|--------|------|
| **Money Avoidance** | "Money is bad/corrupting" | Sabotaging wealth, giving it away impulsively, shame |
| **Money Worship** | "More money will solve everything" | Overspending, workaholism, never enough |
| **Money Status** | "Self-worth = net worth" | Overspending to impress, shame about finances |
| **Money Vigilance** | "Money is private, be careful" | Excessive anxiety, difficulty enjoying money |

**Why this matters for you:**
- Your own money scripts will shape how you approach this transfer
- Your young adults have already absorbed scripts from watching you
- "Teaching" them about money often means transmitting your scripts (including unhelpful ones)

**Reflection prompt:** *What did you learn about money from your parents—not what they said, but what you absorbed from watching them?*

## Part 2: The Psychology of the Receiving Generation (Ages 20-25)

### 2.1 Developmental Stage: Emerging Adulthood

Your young adults are in a distinct developmental phase characterised by:
- **Identity exploration** — "Who am I, really?"
- **Instability** — relationships, work, living situations in flux
- **Self-focus** — necessary for identity formation (not selfishness)
- **Feeling in-between** — not adolescent, not fully adult
- **Possibilities** — future feels open, unwritten

**What they need from you:**
- Autonomy support, not control
- Respect for their emerging identity, even if incomplete
- Tolerance for their experiments and mistakes
- Presence without surveillance

**What feels helpful to you but harmful to them:**
- Detailed guidance on "how to do it right"
- Frequent check-ins on their progress
- Sharing your own financial journey as a template
- Expressing worry about their decisions

### 2.2 How Wealth Complicates Development

Receiving significant wealth during identity formation creates unique challenges:

**Identity confusion:** "Am I successful, or is this just family money?"

**Imposter feelings:** "I didn't earn this. Do I deserve it?"

**Social navigation:** "Do people like me or my money?"

**Comparison trap:** "My friends are working for their money. What does that make me?"

**Paralysis:** "If I make a wrong choice with this money, I've wasted my family's gift."

**The worst thing you can do:** Add pressure for them to be "worthy" of the gift. This amplifies every challenge above.

**The best thing you can do:** Normalise that these feelings exist and create space for them to be expressed without you trying to fix them.

### 2.3 What Young Adults Fear (But Won't Tell You)

Based on research and clinical experience with wealth-receiving young adults:

| Fear | What They Think | What They Say |
|------|-----------------|---------------|
| Disappointing you | "They'll think I wasted it" | "Thanks, I'm being careful" |
| Being surveilled | "They're watching my choices" | "Everything's going great" |
| Losing autonomy | "This money comes with strings" | "I appreciate the guidance" |
| Being ungrateful | "I should feel happier about this" | "I'm so grateful" |
| Not knowing what to do | "I have no idea and I can't admit it" | "I've got a plan" |

**Translation exercise:** When they say something that sounds compliant and confident, ask yourself: *"What might they actually be feeling that they can't say?"*

## Part 3: Language Guide

### 3.1 Phrases to Avoid

| Avoid | Why It's Problematic |
|-------|---------------------|
| "We want to make sure you use it wisely" | Implies surveillance and judgment |
| "This is a big responsibility" | Adds pressure, implies potential failure |
| "We worked hard for this money" | Creates guilt and obligation |
| "We just want to know you're okay" | Feels like monitoring |
| "What are you planning to do with it?" | Demands accountability |
| "Have you thought about...?" | Implies their thinking is insufficient |
| "We trust you, but..." | The "but" negates the trust |
| "Don't waste this opportunity" | Defines success, adds pressure |
| "Let us know if you need advice" | Positions you as authority |
| "We're so proud you're being responsible" | Rewards compliance, creates performance incentive |

### 3.2 Phrases to Use

| Use | Why It Works |
|-----|--------------|
| "This is yours. No strings attached." | Establishes true gift |
| "You don't owe us anything, including updates." | Releases obligation |
| "We're not going to ask about it." | Creates privacy |
| "Whatever you do with it is your choice." | Respects autonomy |
| "There's no wrong answer here." | Reduces paralysis |
| "We've made our own mistakes with money. That's how learning works." | Normalises imperfection |
| "We're here if you ever want to talk, but you don't have to." | Offers without expectation |
| "This might bring up complicated feelings. That's normal." | Validates emotional complexity |
| "We'd rather stay close to you than know what you do with the money." | Prioritises relationship |
| "You might do things we wouldn't do. That's okay." | Grants permission to differentiate |

### 3.3 The Art of the Non-Question

**Asking questions** about money—even well-intentioned ones—can feel like surveillance.

**Practice not asking.** This is harder than it sounds.

When you want to ask "How's the financial stuff going?":
- **Don't ask.** Just be present.
- If they bring it up, listen without advising.
- Tolerate not knowing.

**The gift of not asking** is one of the most powerful things you can offer.

### 3.4 When They Make Choices You Disagree With

They will. Here's how to respond:

**Your internal reaction:** "That's a mistake. They're going to regret that. I should say something."

**What to do instead:**
1. **Pause.** Don't respond immediately.
2. **Ask yourself:** "Is this dangerous, or just different from what I would do?"
3. **If not dangerous:** Say nothing. Tolerate your discomfort.
4. **If they ask for input:** Ask "Do you want my perspective, or do you want me to just listen?"
5. **If you must speak:** "I might do it differently, but it's your call and I support you figuring it out."

**What to never do:**
- Mention it repeatedly
- Bring it up with other family members
- Use it as evidence later ("Remember when you...")
- Let it affect your warmth toward them

## Part 4: Delivery Options for Education

### 4.1 Overview of Approaches

| Approach | Description | Pros | Cons | Best For |
|----------|-------------|------|------|----------|
| **No formal education** | Gift with no accompanying program | Maximum autonomy, no emotional debt | No support if they want it | Highly differentiated young adults; families with open communication |
| **Materials only (self-directed)** | Workbooks/resources provided privately | Private, self-paced, low pressure | Low completion rates; may feel like homework | Young adults who like self-directed learning |
| **Independent facilitator** | Professional (not family-connected) offers optional sessions | Expertise, neutrality, confidentiality | Cost; stranger barrier; engagement not guaranteed | Families where internal dynamics are complex |
| **Family-delivered** | Parents/grandparents deliver education | Connection; low cost | High emotional debt risk; surveillance dynamics; expertise questions | Only if family has exceptional communication and young adults genuinely want it |
| **Hybrid** | Materials + optional facilitator + optional family conversation | Flexibility; meets different preferences | Complexity; family involvement risks | Families willing to truly let young adults choose their path |

### 4.2 Recommended Approach: Phased and Participant-Led

**Phase 0: Permission Check**
- Before designing anything, an independent party (not family) asks each young adult: "What would actually help you? What would feel like pressure?"
- Accept their answers, even if they say "nothing"

**Phase 1: Minimal Offer**
- Private workbook or reflection guide delivered to each person individually
- Explicit statement: "This is optional. No one will ask about it."
- No group component unless requested
- No timeline or completion expectation

**Phase 2: Responsive Expansion (Only If Requested)**
- If one person wants to talk to someone: offer independent facilitator
- If multiple want to discuss together: offer facilitated session
- Expansion is pull-based, not push-based

**What Not to Do:**
- Design an elaborate program and present it as a gift
- Schedule family meetings to "discuss the money"
- Create expectations of participation
- Ask for updates on engagement

### 4.3 If You Must Be Involved

If family involvement is culturally important or genuinely desired by your young adults, minimise harm by:

1. **Ask, don't assume.** "Would you like us involved, or would you prefer to do this independently?"
2. **Offer, don't require.** "We're available if helpful, but no pressure."
3. **Be transparent about your own scripts.** "I know I tend to be anxious about money. That's my stuff, not yours."
4. **Separate the money from the education.** "The money is yours regardless. The learning is optional."
5. **Accept if they decline.** Without guilt trips, repeat offers, or visible disappointment.

### 4.4 Working With an Independent Facilitator

If you engage a professional (financial therapist, wealth counsellor, coach), ensure:

| Requirement | Why |
|-------------|-----|
| **Confidentiality** | They report nothing to family without explicit consent |
| **No agenda** | They don't have outcomes family has defined |
| **Young adult chooses** | Whether and when to engage |
| **Clear payment separation** | If family pays, make clear facilitator serves young adult, not family |
| **Credentials** | Look for: CFT (Certified Financial Therapist), wealth psychology training, family systems background |

**Red flags in facilitators:**
- "I'll keep you updated on their progress"
- "I'll make sure they understand your values"
- "We'll get them on track"

## Part 5: Resources for Further Learning

### 5.1 Books

**On Wealth Psychology:**
- *Mind Over Money* — Brad Klontz & Ted Klontz
  - Core text on money scripts and financial psychology
- *The Psychology of Money* — Morgan Housel
  - Accessible essays on behavior and wealth
- *Strangers in Paradise* — James Grubman
  - Specifically about wealth receivers navigating identity

**On Family Systems:**
- *The Dance of Intimacy* — Harriet Lerner
  - Accessible introduction to differentiation
- *Family Evaluation* — Michael Kerr & Murray Bowen
  - Deeper dive into Bowen family systems (more clinical)

**On Emerging Adulthood:**
- *Emerging Adulthood* — Jeffrey Arnett
  - Understanding the 18-25 developmental stage
- *The Defining Decade* — Meg Jay
  - What young adults actually face (read to understand, not to prescribe)

**On Giving Well:**
- *The Gift* — Lewis Hyde
  - Philosophical exploration of gift-giving dynamics
- *Connected Fates, Separate Destinies* — Fanny Duckert
  - Norwegian research on wealth inheritance psychology

### 5.2 Podcasts & Audio

- **"The Mind of Money"** — Various episodes on wealth psychology
- **"Hidden Brain"** — Episodes on money, gifts, family dynamics
- **"Where Should We Begin?"** — Esther Perel (for understanding relational dynamics)

### 5.3 Professional Resources (Australia)

| Type | Organisation/Credential | Notes |
|------|------------------------|-------|
| Financial Therapists | Financial Therapy Association (US-based but has Australian members) | Look for CFT credential |
| Family Business Advisors | Family Business Australia | If wealth is business-connected |
| Wealth Counsellors | Various; check credentials | Ensure psychology training, not just financial |
| Family Systems Therapists | AAFT, PACFA | Bowen-trained ideal |

### 5.4 Questions to Ask Professionals

Before engaging anyone:
1. "What's your approach when family and young adult interests diverge?"
2. "What do you report back to family, if anything?"
3. "How do you handle it if the young adult doesn't want to engage?"
4. "What training do you have in family systems / wealth psychology specifically?"
5. "What does success look like to you in this kind of work?"

**Listen for:** Young-adult-centered, autonomy-respecting, non-prescriptive answers.
**Beware of:** Compliance-focused, family-agenda-serving, outcome-driven answers.

## Part 6: Self-Assessment for the Giving Generation

### 6.1 Honest Reflection Prompts

Answer these privately. Don't share with your young adults—they're for your own preparation.

**On Your Intentions:**
1. What am I hoping they will do with this money? (Be honest, even if it's "not waste it")
2. How will I feel if they do something completely different from what I hope?
3. Am I offering education because they need it, or because it makes me feel better?
4. What would I lose if I gave the money and then completely stepped back?

**On Your Own Money Story:**
5. What did I learn about money from my parents that I've never questioned?
6. What financial mistakes have I made that I've never shared?
7. What am I afraid my young adults will repeat from my history?
8. What am I trying to protect them from that might be my fear, not their reality?

**On Control:**
9. Can I genuinely accept that they might do things I think are wrong?
10. If they "fail" financially, will I blame myself? Will I blame them?
11. Am I more worried about the money or about them?
12. What's the worst-case scenario I'm trying to prevent? How realistic is it?

**On Relationships:**
13. Would I rather know what they're doing with the money, or have them feel free around me?
14. Can I tolerate not knowing and not asking?
15. If they make choices I disagree with, can I still show warmth?
16. What matters more: the money being used "well" or our relationship being strong?

### 6.2 Readiness Checklist

Before offering any wealth education, check yourself:

| Statement | Honest Answer |
|-----------|---------------|
| I can give this money with truly no strings attached | ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Unsure |
| I can accept if they don't engage with any education | ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Unsure |
| I commit to not asking about their financial choices | ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Unsure |
| I can tolerate them making choices I disagree with | ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Unsure |
| I can keep my anxiety about this private | ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Unsure |
| I won't discuss their choices with other family members | ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Unsure |
| I'm offering education for them, not to manage my own worry | ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Unsure |

**If you answered "No" or "Unsure" to any of these:** You have work to do before proceeding. Consider speaking with a therapist or counsellor about your own relationship with this transfer—not to fix yourself, but to understand yourself.

### 6.3 The Hardest Question

*If they took this money and did the exact opposite of what you hope—spent it all, gave it away, made choices you consider foolish—and they remained happy, confident, and close to you... would that be okay?*

If yes: You're ready.
If no: Sit with why. That's where your work is.

## Part 7: Warning Signs to Watch For

### 7.1 In Yourself

| Warning Sign | What It Might Mean |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Frequently thinking about what they're doing with the money | Struggling to let go |
| Bringing up money in conversations they didn't initiate | Surveillance impulse |
| Feeling hurt that they haven't mentioned it | Expecting gratitude performance |
| Discussing their finances with other family members | Boundary violation |
| Feeling anxious when you don't know their plans | Need for control |
| Comparing their choices to what you would do | Imposing your values |
| Interpreting their silence as ingratitude | Expecting emotional labor |

**If you notice these:** They're normal. Don't act on them. Consider talking to a professional about your own process.

### 7.2 In Your Young Adults

| Warning Sign | What It Might Mean |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Over-thanking, excessive gratitude displays | Performing obligation |
| Telling you exactly what you want to hear | Compliance mode |
| Avoiding family gatherings | Escaping surveillance |
| Sudden alignment with your values/preferences | Suppressing differentiation |
| Inability to make any decisions | Paralysis from pressure |
| Complete silence on the topic | Avoidance due to discomfort |
| One-word answers when you ask anything | Defensive withdrawal |

**If you notice these:** Do not address directly. Instead: back off. Give more space. Stop asking. The signal is that they need distance, not conversation.

### 7.3 In Family Dynamics

| Warning Sign | What It Might Mean |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Extended family asking you about the young adults' finances | Poor boundaries in family system |
| Comparisons between cousins | Competition/hierarchy forming |
| One young adult becoming the "responsible one" | Role assignment, pressure |
| Family gatherings feeling tense around money | System stress |
| Young adults asking each other what they're "supposed to" do | Seeking compliance scripts |

**If you notice these:** The family system needs attention, not just the young adults. Consider family systems therapy or consultation.

## Part 8: A Final Note on Getting It "Right"

You won't get this perfect. That's not the goal.

The goal is to:
1. **Transfer wealth without transferring burden**
2. **Offer support without creating obligation**
3. **Stay connected without surveillance**
4. **Accept outcomes you didn't choose**

Your young adults will make mistakes. So will you. The relationship matters more than the money, and the relationship survives mistakes when there's warmth, respect, and space.

The greatest gift you can give alongside wealth is this:

**The freedom to figure it out themselves, with you nearby but not watching.**
